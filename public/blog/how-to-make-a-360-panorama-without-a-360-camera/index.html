How to Make a 360 Panorama Without a 360 Camera

A dedicated 360 camera is convenient, but it is not the only route. Choose between generating an imagined environment, capturing a real place in multiple shots, and rendering a virtual scene.

By 360 Panorama Viewer · Updated 19 October 2026 · 4 minute read

Choose based on what must be true

If a panorama must document an actual room, capture it. If it needs to illustrate an idea or become a game backdrop, generated content may be suitable. A single photo cannot reveal what was behind the camera. AI can invent those views, but it cannot verify their accuracy.

Method 1: expand a photo with AI

Start with the photo to 360 converter. Choose a clear source image and describe the environment that should continue around it: room style, materials, lighting, surroundings and horizon. Request a full equirectangular environment rather than a wide-angle photograph.

  1. Select the source photo and describe the intended scene.
  2. Generate the panorama in the studio. Generation uses the account's available credits; local viewing and conversion tools are free.
  3. Open the result in the 360 viewer and inspect all directions.
  4. Regenerate or edit if doors, walls, the horizon or the wraparound seam do not continue convincingly.

This works for visual exploration and fictional spaces. For a property listing, survey or other factual record, disclose generated regions and keep the original capture. An attractive result does not establish accurate room dimensions or unseen features.

Method 2: use your phone's panorama mode

A phone sweep is the quickest way to capture a broad horizontal view. Stand in one place, move slowly and keep the camera level. Hold exposure steady when the camera app permits it, and avoid subjects moving through the join between frames.

Check the vertical coverage before calling it a full photo sphere. A complete 360 × 180 image includes the sky overhead and the ground beneath you. Many phone panorama modes capture neither. Treat the result as a partial panorama unless the capture workflow explicitly records a sphere.

A resize cannot fill missing directions. Stretching a narrow panorama into a 2:1 rectangle warps the scene. Use a partial-panorama viewer, capture extra rows in a stitcher, or generate the missing regions with a clear understanding that those regions are synthetic.

Method 3: stitch overlapping photographs

Use a camera or phone with a stitcher that supports spherical output. Capture overlapping views around the horizon, then additional rows above and below it. Include the zenith and nadir. A textured wall is easier to align than a blank sky, so give neighboring frames shared features wherever possible.

Rotate around the lens entrance pupil as closely as the equipment allows. Moving the camera sideways between frames creates parallax: nearby objects shift relative to distant ones, and the stitcher cannot align both at once. Interiors and railings make this especially visible. A panoramic tripod head helps for repeated or demanding captures.

Lock white balance and exposure where practical. Keep people and moving objects away from the joins. Stitch, level the horizon and export a full 2:1 equirectangular image. Preserve a higher-quality master before making a compressed sharing copy.

Method 4: render a virtual scene

If you already have a 3D room, landscape or game scene, render from a single viewpoint using the renderer's panoramic or equirectangular camera mode. Another route is six square views facing the cube axes, with consistent 90-degree fields of view and orientation.

Use the cubemap to equirectangular converter to turn those six display images into a panorama. It changes projection; it does not create HDR values or add missing scene geometry. For lighting, keep a scene-linear HDR export from the renderer alongside the display copy.

Inspect, tag and publish

  1. Inspect the sphere. In the 360 viewer, look behind the initial view, through the seam, straight up and straight down.
  2. Check the layout. A complete sphere normally exports at 2:1. Read the projection guide if the result stretches or folds.
  3. Export for the destination. Use a tagged JPEG for a photo workflow, six faces for a skybox, or an appropriate HDR original for lighting.
  4. Tag the final file. The metadata injector adds panorama information locally. Recheck tags after later edits.

If a Facebook upload still looks flat, follow the seven publishing checks. A working browser preview establishes that the source image can be viewed as a sphere; it does not guarantee that every social uploader will accept it.
